\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the measurements establish.} In this regime (a single shared tied output projection, ${\leq}350$M, undertrained) the standard cosine
conflict diagnostic misreads the CE/MTP interaction. On the rows that carry a
target the per-row gradients are aligned (active-row median cosine ${\approx}0.53$,
${\approx}3\%$ opposed; the full-vocabulary median is $+1$ only because
${\approx}88$--$92\%$ of rows are scalar multiples by construction), the aggregate
is $\approx 0$ or negative, and the two decouple during training. Instrumenting the
per-row norms shows the aggregate is set by a high-norm opposed minority
($\approx0.3\%$ of all rows, carrying $60$--$69\%$ of the mass), not by disjoint
support: the losses load the same rows (norm-profile cosine $0.98$) but oppose on the few rows that
dominate the weighted sum. Crucially, the next-token degradation is large and
unambiguous, yet neutralizing that directional opposition does not remove it: the
surgery baselines in Appendix~\ref{app:surgery} recover nothing, so the opposed
minority the cosine detects is not the fixable lever. The lever that helps is
capacity separation, though in our runs it reduces rather than eliminates the gap.

\paragraph{Two failure modes, one instrument.} The aggregate cosine misleads in
two opposite directions depending on the optimizer, and the paper's central claim
is that both are failures of the \emph{same} diagnostic. Under Muon the aggregate
is ${\approx}{-}0.08$, close enough to zero that a conflict diagnostic reads
``no interference,'' a \emph{false negative}: the next-token loss degrades by
$0.39$ nats regardless. Under AdamW the aggregate is ${\approx}{-}0.28$,
negative enough to trip a conflict flag, but this is a \emph{misleading positive}:
gradient surgery that projects out exactly this opposition recovers nothing
(Appendix~\ref{app:surgery}), because the opposition is mass-dominant yet causally
inert. A practitioner reading only the aggregate would conclude ``safe to add
MTP'' under Muon and ``apply surgery'' under AdamW, and both conclusions are
wrong. The instrument does not merely lose power; it points the wrong way, and
which way depends on an optimizer choice unrelated to whether interference is
present. This is why the per-row and per-norm decomposition, not a scalar
threshold on the aggregate, is what the regime requires.

\paragraph{What they do not establish.} We do not show that MTP \emph{as
practiced} (with separate per-horizon heads) degrades next-token modeling;
our formulation is a single tied head, and separate-head designs give the
auxiliary its own capacity precisely so that the losses cannot compete on shared
rows. We do not establish causation between the norm-weighted opposition and the
loss increase; we show they co-occur and that interventions behave as a
norm-weighting/capacity account predicts (in particular, surgery that removes the
opposition does not remove the degradation). And the effect lives in an
undertrained, small-batch, ${\leq}350$M regime where MTP is already documented to
underperform \citep{zuhri2025top}.

\paragraph{Future work.} Three experiments would broaden the scope from ``the
diagnostic misreads this regime'' toward a causal, general statement: (1) a
\textbf{separate-head MTP} variant, to localize whether the degradation is
intrinsic to head-sharing; (2) \textbf{$n{=}3$ at 350M} plus a 124M CE-only
per-row baseline, to turn the single-seed scale observation into a measured
trend; and (3) \textbf{matched-seed, single-pass gradient surgery} at $n{=}3$, to
remove the numerical-drift caveat. A further question, \emph{which} norm
structure produces the collapse, is settled directly here: instrumenting the per-row norms
(\texttt{measure\_norm\_support.py}, $n{=}3$ seeds per optimizer) shows the two
losses load the same rows (norm-profile cosine $0.98$) while a high-norm opposed
minority carries the majority of the mass (opposed-norm fraction $0.60$/$0.69$),
so the mechanism is norm-weighted cancellation, not support divergence
(Section~\ref{sec:emergent}, Figure~\ref{fig:normsupport}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Cosine similarity between task gradients is the default instrument for detecting
interference in multi-task and auxiliary-loss training. We showed that at a
language model's shared, tied output projection this instrument is misleading:
the per-row CE and MTP gradients are aligned in direction on the rows that carry a
target (active-row median cosine ${\approx}0.53$; the full-vocabulary median is
$+1$ because most rows are scalar multiples by construction), the aggregate cosine
is driven negative by a high-norm opposed minority ($\approx0.3\%$ of rows carrying
most of the mass) rather than by a broad directional conflict, and the two metrics
decouple during training.
Adding the shared auxiliary degrades next-token loss substantially, and
neutralizing the measured opposition does not repair it, so the degradation is
not the directional conflict a cosine diagnostic would report. The aggregate is
set by per-row gradient norms, not by the per-row directions the cosine averages,
a distinction that explains why conflict-targeted surgery does not help here and
why capacity separation does. We deliver the result
on a fully reproducible testbed with a committed statistics script, and we are
explicit about the shared-head, small-scale scope and the experiments that would
extend it.
