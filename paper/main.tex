\begin{abstract}
Cosine similarity between task gradients is the standard instrument for
detecting \emph{conflict} in multi-task and auxiliary-loss training: a negative
cosine is read as interference, and gradient-surgery methods act on that signal.
We show that at a language model's shared, tied output projection (the regime
this paper is scoped to, distinct from the separate-head multi-token prediction
used at scale) this instrument is misleading. Instrumenting the per-row gradients that a next-token (CE) loss
and an auxiliary multi-token (MTP) loss deposit on the output projection, we find
they are near-perfectly \emph{aligned in direction}: the median per-row cosine is
$\approx +1$, only ${\approx}0.3\%$ of rows show directional opposition
($\cos<0$), and $98.4\%$ of rows exceed $|\cos|>0.3$, against a calibrated
control (CE versus an unrelated L1 loss) on the same threshold that reads
${\approx}0.4\%$. Yet the \emph{aggregate} cosine over the flattened matrix sits
near $0$, and we show this decoupling \emph{emerges during training} from an
initial state where both metrics agree at $\approx +0.99$. The aggregate
near-zero is therefore not the cancellation of opposing per-row gradients; it is
consistent with the two losses concentrating their gradient \emph{magnitude} on
\emph{different output rows} (\emph{support divergence}; inferred from aligned
per-row directions plus a near-zero aggregate, not directly measured; see
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Adding the shared MTP objective degrades next-token loss substantially
($+0.39$ nats at 124M, Welch's $t$, $p\approx 8\times10^{-5}$), but this
degradation co-occurs with aligned, not conflicting, gradients. We further
report that the high per-row alignment is \emph{general} across the model's weight
matrices, persists when the head is \emph{untied}, and that rarer tokens show
\emph{higher} per-row alignment than common ones; and that no capacity-separated
auxiliary we tried recovers the CE-only baseline (the closest, a tuned
contrastive variant, is statistically indistinguishable from baseline at $n{=}3$,
$p{\approx}0.19$, but its point estimate still sits above it: non-recovery, not
a demonstrated recovery). The methodological
consequence is direct: in this regime, cosine-based conflict diagnostics measure
the wrong axis, because the operative axis is gradient \emph{support}, not direction.
Our account complements \citet{godey2026lmhead}, who characterize how the output
head suppresses a \emph{single} loss's gradient \emph{norm}; we characterize, in
direction-space, what a cosine diagnostic sees when \emph{two} losses share it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Predicting several future tokens at once (multi-token prediction, MTP) has
become a common ingredient in large-scale language modeling, used to improve
sample efficiency and to enable self-speculative decoding
\citep{gloeckle2024mtp,deepseekv3}. The auxiliary objective is double-edged: a
growing body of work reports that adding it can \emph{degrade} the primary
next-token objective, particularly at smaller scales and longer horizons
\citep{gloeckle2024mtp,zuhri2025top}. Asked why, practitioners reach for the
intuition that pervades multi-task learning: the two losses must pull the shared
parameters in \emph{conflicting directions}, and the remedy is to detect and
neutralize that conflict, typically from the sign of a gradient cosine
\citep{yu2020pcgrad,wang2021gradvaccine}. This intuition has been questioned in
auxiliary-task learning: \citet{jiang2023forkmerge} report that gradient-conflict
coordination can overlook auxiliary-target generalization, so the cosine signal
does not by itself determine negative transfer. We ask what that signal actually
measures at a language model's shared output head.

This paper is a measurement study of that intuition for the case of an auxiliary
multi-token objective sharing a model's tied output projection. Our central
finding is diagnostic: \textbf{the cosine conflict signal is misleading here.} We
instrument the gradient each loss deposits on the output projection and examine
it \textbf{row by row} (one row per vocabulary item). Two facts emerge, both from
data already in hand. First, the per-row gradients are not opposed; they are
\emph{aligned}: the median per-row cosine sits at $\approx +1$ across the
measured window (the first 1000 steps of a dedicated 124M diagnostic run, and out
to 87k steps at 350M; Section~\ref{sec:aligned}), and ${\approx}0.3\%$ of rows
show negative cosine, while $98.4\%$ exceed $|\cos|>0.3$ against a control (CE
versus an unrelated regularizer) that reads ${\approx}0.4\%$ on the same
threshold. Second,
the \emph{aggregate} cosine over the flattened matrix is nonetheless near zero,
and tracking it over training shows the two metrics \textbf{start together} (both
$\approx +0.99$ at initialization) and then \textbf{decouple}: within ${\sim}200$
steps the aggregate falls below zero while the per-row median stays at
$\approx +1$. The natural reading of an aggregate near zero (that opposing
per-row gradients cancel) cannot hold here, because there is almost nothing to
cancel. A cancellation-free way to obtain a near-zero aggregate while per-row
directions agree is for the two losses to place their gradient \emph{magnitude}
on \emph{different rows}: direction agrees, support diverges.

We frame this \textbf{support divergence} as the axis a cosine diagnostic cannot
see (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}), and we relate it to the observed degradation
without claiming to have isolated its cause. Adding the shared MTP objective degrades next-token loss by
${\approx}0.39$ nats (${\approx}10\%$) at 124M, a large and unambiguous effect
(Welch's $t$, $p\approx 8\times10^{-5}$), yet it co-occurs with aligned, not
conflicting, per-row gradients (the degradation is measured on the full
30{,}517-step run; per-row alignment on the diagnostic runs above). Three further
observations, all from committed
runs, are consistent with a capacity/support account rather than a
directional-conflict one. (i) Conflict-targeted gradient surgery does not recover
the baseline, as expected when there is negligible directional conflict to
remove (Appendix~\ref{app:surgery}). (ii) Detaching the auxiliary's gradient from
the shared head (stop-gradient) makes the damage \emph{worse}, not better. (iii)
Giving the auxiliary its own capacity (a layer-split contrastive auxiliary)
reduces but does \emph{not} eliminate the gap: neither the standard nor a
hyperparameter-tuned variant recovers the CE-only baseline within noise when
aggregated across seeds.

These findings carry a methodological point beyond MTP. Cosine-based conflict
detection is a workhorse of multi-task and auxiliary-learning optimization
\citep{yu2020pcgrad,wang2021gradvaccine,du2018auxsim}, and it is the wrong
instrument in this regime: it reports no conflict, yet interference is real. The
decomposition that exposes the problem separates \textbf{direction} (cosine) from
\textbf{support} (where each gradient's magnitude lives). Two additional
measurements sharpen the picture and guard against over-reading it. The high
per-row alignment is \textbf{general} across the network's weight matrices (attention and MLP matrices show it too, many with strongly \emph{negative}
aggregate cosine), so the output head is not special because the metric singles
it out; it is consequential because it directly produces the logits. And the
alignment \textbf{persists when the head is untied}, so it is a property of the
output-projection gradient, not an artifact of weight tying. We also observe that
\emph{rarer} tokens exhibit \emph{higher} per-row alignment than common ones,
under both optimizers. Finally, our account is a direction-space, multi-loss
complement to \citet{godey2026lmhead}, who show that backpropagating a
\emph{single} loss through the low-rank output head destroys most of its gradient
\emph{norm}; we ask what a \emph{cosine} diagnostic reports when \emph{two} losses
share that head, and answer that their per-row directions align while their
support appears to compete: an inference from aligned directions plus a
near-zero aggregate, which a direct per-row-norm measurement would confirm.

We are explicit about scope. Every result is for a \textbf{single shared tied
output head}, at 124M ($n{=}5$) and 350M (single seed), undertrained, small-batch.
We do \emph{not} claim that multi-token prediction \emph{as practiced} (separate
per-horizon heads) degrades next-token modeling; establishing that would require a
separate-head experiment we identify as the primary future step
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), along with $n{=}3$ at 350M and matched-seed
surgery. What we do establish, on data in hand, is that the standard cosine
conflict diagnostic misreads this regime, and why.

\paragraph{Contributions.} All on a controlled, reproducible GPT-2 testbed (124M
primary, 350M illustrative; FineWeb) with open code and a committed statistics
script:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A diagnostic result (Figures~\ref{fig:perrow},~\ref{fig:emergent}).}
  For an auxiliary multi-token objective sharing the tied output projection, the
  per-row gradients of the two losses are near-perfectly \emph{aligned in
  direction} (median cosine $\approx +1$; ${\approx}0.3\%$ of rows opposed,
  against a $0.42\%$ calibrated control) while the aggregate cosine is $\approx 0$,
  and this decoupling \emph{emerges during training} from an aligned start. Standard
  cosine-based conflict diagnostics therefore \emph{misdiagnose} this regime.
\item \textbf{A norm decomposition of the collapse (Figure~\ref{fig:normdecomp}).}
  Via an exact identity, we show the aggregate would read $+0.94$/$+0.96$ if
  gradient magnitude were flat across rows but reads $-0.08$/$-0.28$: the collapse
  is set by per-row \emph{norm} structure, not by the typical per-row direction.
  We ship the instrument (\texttt{measure\_norm\_support.py}) that logs per-row
  norms to complete the support-versus-opposed-minority test in one short pass.
\item \textbf{A calibrated, general characterization
  (Figures~\ref{fig:perlayer},~\ref{fig:tokenfreq}).} The alignment is
  \textbf{general across the network} and \textbf{survives untying} the head, and
  rarer tokens align \emph{more}. The next-token degradation is large and
  significant ($+0.39$ nats, $p\approx 8\times10^{-5}$) yet co-occurs with aligned
  gradients.
\item \textbf{Intervention evidence for a support/capacity account
  (Figure~\ref{fig:interventions}, Appendix~\ref{app:surgery}).}
  Conflict-targeted surgery does not recover the baseline; stop-gradient makes the
  damage worse; and a capacity-separated auxiliary reduces but does not eliminate
  the gap (neither standard nor tuned recovers within noise), each reported with
  its statistical test and caveats.
\item \textbf{A bridge to the output-layer bottleneck literature.} We position the
  result as the direction-space, multi-loss complement to
  \citet{godey2026lmhead}'s single-loss, norm-space account of the output head as
  a gradient bottleneck.
\end{itemize}
